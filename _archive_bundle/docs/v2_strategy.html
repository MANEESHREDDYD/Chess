Design and Build Plan · v2 · Supersedes v1

MIRRORA Staged MVP

A chess game where the final opponent is built from the way you play. Built as a PWA, validated in stages, on free tiers, with AI agents.
Mode One of Seven Maneesh Reddy Duddukunta May 2026

Three pressure tests on the previous design bible reached the same conclusion: the document specified a twelve-week production for an assumption that has not been tested once. This document responds by re-architecting the entire build as three staged gates, where Stage 0 is a weekend prototype with a single job — verify whether the Mirror feels like the player. Everything that follows is contingent on that verdict.

What the three pressure tests collectively said

The Mirror may not be magical. Maia already provides human-like chess. A perturbed Stockfish weighted by a sixteen-dimensional style vector may produce an opponent that oscillates between brilliance and blunder rather than one that feels like the player. The hook of the entire seven-mode franchise rests on this working.

At low Elo, style vectors converge. Players rated 1000–1500 — eighty percent of the addressable market — blunder so much that their style data looks similar. The Mirror may be distinguishable only for players above 1800, who are not the commercial market.

Scope is overbuilt. Nineteen chapters, fifteen characters, Blender procedural pipeline, multiplayer, analysis suite, three platforms — designed before any evidence that the central mechanic resonates.

The hook is buried. The first Mirror reveal at Chapter VII sits behind three to four hours of story. The chess audience has a median session length of twelve minutes. Most users will churn before they ever see the product's central promise.

The Mahabharata framing narrows the market. Resonant for South Asian players (lower willingness to pay) and friction for Western players (higher willingness to pay). The framing must be optional, not mandatory.

What this document changes

  1. Three stages, two kill criteria. Stage 0 is a weekend prototype. If it fails, the project ends. Stage 1 is a ten-week thin slice with a Beta cohort and behavioral acceptance criteria. If it fails, no Stage 2. Stage 2 is the full Mode 1 — only if Stage 1 evidence justifies it.
  2. PWA-first, not native. Single web codebase served by Cloudflare Pages free tier. Works on iOS, Android, Windows, and desktop browsers. No app store fees, no native build complexity, faster iteration.
  3. The Mirror is surfaced early. The first Mirror reveal moves from Chapter VII to Chapter III, within the first ninety minutes of play. The Mahabharata framing becomes an optional theme toggle. Default piece set is standard chess; Mahabharata is a skin.
  4. The Mirror is tunable. After the first Mirror match, the player is asked a single direct question — "did that play like you?" — and can adjust the style vector by reweighting dimensions. This addresses the low-Elo signal problem by letting the player co-create their reflection rather than receive it.
  5. A second AI agent: the Coach. Separate from the Mirror. The Coach is a teaching agent that engages every player after every game, identifies their weakest tactical motif, and serves personalized puzzles in a character voice. Powered by Gemini's free tier. Serves the requirement that the product help beginners learn and skilled players improve.
  6. Free-tier infrastructure throughout. Cloudflare Pages (frontend hosting, free unlimited bandwidth), Supabase free tier (database, auth, realtime, 50K MAU ceiling), Stockfish WASM (client-side, free), Maia weights (open source via lc0 WASM), Gemini API free tier (1,500 requests per day for Coach text generation), Blender Studio (free for asset generation). Total recurring infrastructure cost at MVP: $0.
  7. GPL compliance baked in. Stockfish and Maia are GPLv3. The PWA bundles them as separate WASM modules called via interface, distributes them with proper notices, links to source. The application code remains under the user's chosen license.

Section One

The Three-Stage Strategy— gated by behavioral evidence, not by hope.

A weekend prototype gates a ten-week build, which gates the complete Mode 1 release. Each stage has a single primary question, a behavioral pass criterion, and a kill criterion that is honored if the data says so.

Stage 0 Mirror Prototype 2 weekends · $0
Stage 1 Hook Test 8–10 weeks · <$200
Stage 2 Complete Mode 1 10–14 weeks · <$2,000

Why staging is non-negotiable

A solo developer with AI agents and a free-tier stack can ship surprising amounts of code. That changes the build economics but not the product economics. The Mirror is the product. If it does not feel like the player to a meaningful fraction of users, no amount of art, story, character work, or analysis tooling redeems the central claim of the game. Three independent pressure tests converged on this. The honest response is to build the smallest possible test of that claim before building anything else.

What this strategy explicitly cuts

Cut from the v1 bible — not in Stage 0 or Stage 1
  • The full nineteen-chapter Mahabharata story arc
  • Twelve of the fifteen named characters (only Vyasa, Yudhishthira, and the Mirror appear in Stage 1)
  • Procedural Blender pipeline for all assets (Stage 1 uses placeholder or AI-generated 2D art)
  • Multiplayer modes — Pilgrim's Path, Sealed Scroll, 2v2 Twin Match
  • The Library of Verses, the Sacred River, the Apprentice's Journal as separate features (only a minimal Vyasa's Review ships in Stage 1)
  • Native iOS, Android, Windows builds (PWA covers all platforms)
  • Audio and music (deferred to Stage 2, AI-generated)
  • The $14.99 purchase model and subscription tier (Stage 0 and Stage 1 are free)
  • The cultural consultant review (Krishna does not appear until Stage 2; the AI agent handles initial scriptwriting then a human reviewer is engaged before Chapter XII script lockdown)
  • Daily Mirror, Echo Tournaments, Style Wars — all deferred to Stage 2

Everything cut from Stage 0 and Stage 1 is restored in Stage 2 if Stage 1 passes. The cuts are not permanent — they are deferrals against unvalidated assumptions.

Section Two

Stage 0— The Mirror Prototype. Two weekends. Free. No story, no theme, no characters.

A bare web page where a user plays a short calibration, then plays a chess opponent generated from their calibration data. They are asked one question: did that play like you? That is the entire product. It exists to answer the single question that determines whether everything else gets built.

Stage 0 — The Mirror Prototype
2 weekends · $0 · Web-only

User flow

  1. Landing page. One screen. Heading: "Play a chess opponent built from how you play." One button: "Begin." No signup. Anonymous session ID stored in browser.
  2. Calibration. Eight tasks. Twelve to fifteen minutes total. Tactical puzzles, opening choices, endgame positions, a short game against Stockfish at depth 5. Progress indicator: "Task 3 of 8."
  3. Style vector computed. Browser-side. Eight to ten dimensions, focused on signal-rich features (opening repertoire, time pressure, exchange willingness, blunder phase, tactical motifs missed). Visualized as a simple radar chart with axis labels in plain English: "You play attacking openings. You take time on every move. You miss forks more than pins."
  4. Mirror generated. Stockfish at depth 12, perturbation function applied. Displayed to user as: "Your Mirror is ready. It is rated around [Elo]. It plays in your style."
  5. First match. Standard chess. Ten minutes per side. The user plays their Mirror. Default piece set: standard Staunton chess pieces. No Mahabharata, no story, no character.
  6. Self-recognition check. After the match, present three unlabeled style profiles: the user's Mirror, a generic Maia 1500, and a generic Stockfish at perturbed depth. Ask: "Which one was you?" This is the behavioral test of whether the Mirror is distinguishable.
  7. Open feedback. A single textarea: "What did your Mirror get right? What did it get wrong?" Submission optional.
  8. Tunable adjustment. Show the player their style vector. Let them reweight any axis up or down ("I am more aggressive than this says"). Regenerate the Mirror. Offer a second match.
  9. End screen. Brief: "Thank you. If you would like to play your Mirror again as it learns from more games, leave your email." Optional email capture for the Stage 1 beta cohort.

Pass criteria — behavioral, measured

Stage 0 runs as an open beta posted to r/chess, r/IndieDev, Hacker News, and chess Twitter. Target: 200–500 completed sessions in two weeks.

  • Primary: ≥30% of sessions submit unprompted feedback that uses language like "felt like me," "knew my weaknesses," "played my openings," or equivalent.
  • Confirmatory: Self-recognition rate ≥60% on the three-profile triangulation (chance is 33%, so this is a meaningful signal).
  • Indicator of demand: ≥15% of sessions leave an email for the Stage 1 cohort.
  • Quality signal: ≥3 chess players rated 1600+ (verified by Chess.com or Lichess handle voluntarily disclosed) post about the prototype publicly.
Kill criterion

If feedback consistently uses words like "generic," "Stockfish," "could be anyone," or "boring opponent," the Mirror fails the core test. Do not iterate on the Mirror for another sprint hoping it improves. The pressure tests warned specifically that perturbed Stockfish may produce uncanny-valley chess rather than human-feeling chess. If that prediction holds, the entire seven-mode design depends on a mechanic that does not work. End the project, or pivot to the post-game analysis tool variant ("show me how an AI of myself would have played differently") which is a different and smaller product.

Honest probability assessment

Based on prior art — Maia's own results, perturbation literature in chess engines, the failure history of personalized chess products — Stage 0 has a meaningful chance of failing. Honest range:

Stage 0 passes both primary and confirmatory criteria:
~25–35%

That probability is low enough that committing to a twelve-week build before testing is irresponsible. It is high enough that the two-weekend test is worth doing. This is the math of the staged strategy.

Section Three

Stage 1— The Hook Test. Eight to ten weeks if Stage 0 passes. The thin slice.

A free PWA with calibration, three chapters, the first Mirror reveal at chapter three (not seven), the Coach agent for personalized teaching, and the Mahabharata theme as an optional toggle. Tested with a beta cohort of 200–500 invited users. Built almost entirely with AI coding agents on free-tier infrastructure.

Stage 1 — The Hook Test
8–10 weeks · <$200 · Free PWA

The thin-slice content

  1. Vyasa's Test (calibration). Eight tasks, fifteen minutes. Sets the player's initial style vector and difficulty band.
  2. Chapter I — Hastinapura. Vyasa accepts the player. A gentle tutorial match against Vidura (Maia 1300 baseline). Twenty minutes of guided play.
  3. Chapter II — Indraprastha. The player meets Yudhishthira and plays him. Maia 1700 with the no-swindle constraint. Twenty-five minutes.
  4. Chapter III — The First Mirror. Vyasa reveals the academy's project. The Mirror appears. Match plays. Self-recognition prompt. Post-match Coach intervention to discuss the result. Forty-five minutes total.
  5. Coach loop unlocked. After Chapter III, the player can return any time to play their Mirror again, or to run a Coach session (personalized puzzles based on weaknesses identified in matches played).
  6. End screen. "More chapters coming. Leave your email." Drives a waitlist for Stage 2.

Section Five

The Mirror, Honestly Engineered— addressing the low-Elo signal problem and the perturbed-Stockfish uncanny valley.

The pressure tests were specific about two failure modes: style vectors converge at low Elo, and perturbed Stockfish does not feel human. The Mirror's design responds to both with concrete countermeasures.

The signal problem at low Elo, and how the Mirror compensates

Players rated 1000–1500 blunder enough that their move distributions look statistically similar. A naive sixteen-dimension style vector cannot meaningfully separate them. The countermeasure is not "more dimensions" — it is fewer, higher-signal dimensions, plus a hybrid model.

Stage 0 and Stage 1 Mirror — eight high-signal dimensions

  1. Opening repertoire (top 3 White, top 3 Black). Most distinctive at any Elo. A 1100 who plays the King's Gambit is not the same as a 1100 who plays the Caro-Kann.
  2. Average move time. Some players blitz, some think. Strong signal, easy to capture.
  3. Time-pressure blunder rate. Moves played with under five seconds on the clock that lose ≥150 cp. Distinctive by player.
  4. Exchange willingness. Proportion of equal trades accepted versus declined.
  5. Preferred minor piece. Knight-keeper vs bishop-keeper, derived from trade history.
  6. Tactical motif blindness. From calibration: the player's miss rate on forks, pins, skewers, removing-the-defender. The Mirror exploits the weakest two motifs.
  7. Endgame technique. Win rate from K+R, K+Q, K+P advantage positions in calibration endgames.
  8. Pawn-structure preference. Open positions vs closed, derived from opening choice.

The hybrid Mirror — Maia plus perturbed Stockfish, not perturbed Stockfish alone

This addresses the uncanny-valley concern directly. A pure perturbed Stockfish oscillates between brilliant and weak in alien ways. The hybrid:

  • Base layer: The Maia model closest to the player's calibration Elo (Maia 1100, 1300, 1500, 1700, or 1900). This provides naturally human move selection.
  • Personalization layer: The Maia move policy is re-ranked by the player's style vector. The vector boosts moves that match the player's opening repertoire and tactical preferences, and demotes moves that the player would not consider.
  • Exploitation layer: In ~20% of positions where the Mirror has an opportunity to test the player's known weakness (a fork the player misses, a pin the player falls for), it switches to a perturbed Stockfish line that creates that position.

This three-layer architecture means the Mirror plays like a human at the player's Elo most of the time, plays like the player specifically in opening and middlegame style choices, and occasionally creates positions designed to expose the player's blind spots — which is what makes it feel like a coach as much as an opponent.

The tunable Mirror — closing the gap with player feedback

After the first Mirror match, the player sees their style vector visualized as a radar. Each axis has a single-line plain-English description: "You play attacking openings." The player can drag any axis up or down to disagree with the model. "I am more careful than this says." The Mirror regenerates immediately. The next match plays with the adjusted vector.

This is the answer to the low-Elo signal problem. If the data alone cannot distinguish two 1200-rated players, the player's own self-knowledge can. Co-creating the Mirror with explicit feedback turns a unilateral inference problem into a collaborative model. It also makes the Mirror feel like a conversation rather than a verdict.

Self-recognition test — the only behavioral check that matters

After a Mirror match, present three unlabeled opponents in card form, each described by their style vector: the player's own Mirror, a generic Maia at the player's Elo, and a different real player's Mirror selected from the cohort. The player picks "which one was you." Chance baseline is 33%. The acceptance criterion is ≥60% correct identification. This is the cleanest measurement of whether the Mirror is meaningfully personalized.

Section Six

The Coach— a teaching agent that engages every player after every game.

Separate from the Mirror. The Mirror is the player; the Coach is a teacher. The Coach serves the requirement that the game help beginners learn and skilled players improve — a requirement Chess.com, Chessable, and existing chess products meet only partially because their teaching is generic. The Coach is personalized to the player's specific games.

The Coach's responsibilities

  1. Post-game intervention. After every game (story, Mirror, free play, or future multiplayer), the Coach appears in character voice and selects one specific moment from the game to discuss. The moment is chosen by Stockfish's analysis — the highest centipawn-loss blunder or the largest missed brilliancy.
  2. Personalized puzzle generation. The Coach identifies the player's two weakest tactical motifs (from the running tally across all games) and generates a five-puzzle drill set targeting those specific motifs. Puzzles are drawn from a curated puzzle database keyed by motif tag.
  3. Lesson delivery. When a player has lost the same way three times — the same opening trap, the same endgame failure, the same time-pressure blunder pattern — the Coach surfaces a focused lesson with three example positions and a one-paragraph explanation.
  4. Adaptive difficulty. The Coach tracks the player's progress on weak motifs and gradually increases puzzle difficulty as accuracy improves. Mastered motifs are deprioritized; new weaknesses are picked up.
  5. Encouragement and pacing. Sessions never exceed twenty minutes by default. The Coach prompts breaks. This is a deliberate anti-pattern against chess platforms that optimize for time-on-task.

Technical implementation

Move analysis
Stockfish at depth 14 over every move in every game. Classification by standard centipawn-loss thresholds. Motif tagging via a lightweight tactical classifier (open source).
Coach voice generation
Google Gemini 1.5 Flash via the free API (1,500 requests/day at Stage 1 scale). One call per Coach intervention. Prompts engineered against a character profile (default: Drona, the teacher from the Mahabharata; alternate voice: a neutral "Coach" persona for players using the Standard theme).
Puzzle bank
Lichess open puzzle database (4M+ puzzles, public domain). Filtered by motif tag, rating, and themes. No proprietary content needed.
Personal weakness model
Local SQLite (synced to Supabase) tracking the player's history of motif failures with rolling counts and decay.
Daily limit
One Coach intervention per game per player, capped at five Coach sessions per day in free tier. Hard limit enforced client-side; soft prompt: "Drona suggests rest. Come back tomorrow."

Sample Coach interaction (Apprentice band, after a fork blunder)

After the Yudhishthira match · Coach: Drona

"Move sixteen. You moved the knight from f3 to d4. The knight was attacked by Yudhishthira's bishop, but the bishop was also defending his queen. You did not see that your own bishop on g5 could have captured the queen instead. This is a fork pattern. It is the third time in your last ten games. We will work on this now."

[ Five fork puzzles offered. Each ~30 seconds. Drona returns at the end with feedback. ]

Why the Coach is not optional

The Mirror tests whether the game's central artistic vision works. The Coach tests whether the game has a reason to exist for the chess audience that is not story-driven. Chess.com keeps players because it helps them improve. Without a serious teaching system, this product is a curiosity. With one, it has a chance to compete on its own terms — not as a Chess.com replacement, but as a complement that knows the player personally and teaches accordingly.

The pressure tests said: this product sits between markets and satisfies neither. The Coach is how the chess-improvement market is served without abandoning the artistic vision.

  1. Play. Story chapter resume, Mirror match, free practice against any unlocked character.
  2. Coach. Personalized puzzles, motif lessons, progress tracker.
  3. Library. Stage 2: opening repertoire, game review. Stage 1: empty, "coming soon."
  4. Profile. Style vector visualization, calibration redo, theme toggle, settings.
  5. About. Credits, GPL notices, source links for Stockfish and Maia, privacy.

Motion principles

  • Piece movement: 240ms ease-in-out. Pieces glide. No physics simulation, no battle animations.
  • Capture: captured piece fades out 180ms while the capturing piece arrives.
  • Check: crimson pulse on the king's square, 1-second sine, three cycles.
  • Promotion: pawn rises 200ms, promoted piece descends 200ms, gold particle burst.
  • Mirror reveal cutscene: a full-screen 3-second sequence where the standard board fades, the player's pieces remain, and a second set of identical pieces appears in twilight light across the board. The visual makes the point without dialogue.
Section Nine

Technical Architecture— PWA, free tier, GPL-compliant.

A single web codebase served as a Progressive Web App. Stockfish and Maia run in Web Workers. Game state and style vectors persist locally and sync to Supabase. The Coach agent calls Gemini's free API. No native builds, no app store fees, no recurring infrastructure cost.

Why this stack

PWA over native
One codebase serves iOS Safari, Android Chrome, Desktop Chrome, Edge, and Firefox. PWA installable on home screen on iOS 16.4+ and all Android. No App Store review delay, no fees, no native build complexity. iOS PWA limitations (no push notifications until iOS 16.4, limited background) are acceptable for a chess game.
Stockfish.wasm
The strongest open-source chess engine. WASM build runs at depth 14 in ~50–100ms on midrange hardware. GPLv3 — bundled as a separate Web Worker module, called via UCI-style message interface. Application code is not a derivative work.
Maia via lc0.wasm
Maia weights run on Leela Chess Zero. lc0 has a WASM build. Maia weight files are small (~50MB). Cached in the service worker so first-load is the only download cost. Required for character authenticity and the hybrid Mirror.
Supabase free tier
50,000 monthly active users, 500MB Postgres, 1GB file storage. Auth, realtime channels, Edge Functions. More than enough for Stage 0 and Stage 1.
Gemini API free
1,500 requests per day, 15 requests per minute, 1M tokens per minute. The Coach generates ~200 tokens per intervention. At 1,500 active users per day with one Coach call each, this is on the edge of the limit; in practice, far below that until Stage 2.
Cloudflare Pages
Free static hosting with unlimited bandwidth and global CDN. Zero cost regardless of traffic.
Cloudflare R2
10GB free storage, zero egress fees. Character portraits, background images, future audio.

GPL compliance plan

Stockfish and Maia are GPLv3. The PWA must comply. The approach follows the standard pattern used by Chess.com, Lichess, and other commercial chess applications:

  1. Separate modules. Stockfish.wasm and lc0.wasm are loaded as Web Workers via separate <script> tags or worker imports. The application code does not link or compile against the engine source. The relationship is "mere aggregation" under GPL — allowed.
  2. Notices. The About screen and a footer link visibly attribute Stockfish (with GPLv3 notice and a link to the source repository) and Maia (with citation of the CSSLab paper and a link to the Maia GitHub repository).
  3. Source availability. Both Stockfish and lc0 are publicly available. The PWA's About page links to both source repositories. No copy is needed on the PWA's own servers, but a copy of each binary is downloadable from a static URL the application controls (Cloudflare R2 mirror).
  4. Application license. The PWA's own source code is under whatever license Maneesh chooses. The Stockfish and Maia inclusions do not impose GPL on the rest of the codebase under this aggregation pattern.

Section Ten

Blender Asset Pipeline— deferred to Stage 2. Specification preserved for continuity.

Stage 0 and Stage 1 use SVG chess pieces and AI-generated 2D character portraits. Blender procedural rendering is a Stage 2 investment, made only after Stage 1 evidence justifies the production cost.

Why Blender is in Stage 2, not Stage 1

A 25-year graphic designer would say: do not build an asset pipeline before you know what you are rendering. Stage 1 ships with the simplest possible visuals — SVG pieces drawn in Figma, character portraits generated via Midjourney or DALL-E with consistent prompts, backgrounds composited from AI generation. Total Stage 1 asset cost: under $50 for AI tool credits, or zero if using free tier services.

If Stage 1 passes its acceptance criteria, the visual quality bar for the commercial release rises, and Blender becomes worth the investment. The previous bible's procedural pipeline spec is preserved without changes for Stage 2 — chess piece generators, character rigs, palette functions. The only change is timing.

Stage 2 pipeline (preserved from v1)

Chess pieces
Procedural generators in Python, one per piece type per faction. Renders to PNG sprites at three angles and four LODs. Bundled in the PWA as static assets.
Characters
One base rig, parameterized customization scripts per character. Four emotion variants per character. Rendered as PNG portraits at 1024×1280px.
Environments
Ten backgrounds total for Mode 1, one per chapter setting type. Rendered at 2560×1440px.
Output
PNG with alpha, bundled into PWA assets at build time. Optional glTF export for a future native 3D path.

A sample Blender Python generator for a Pandava chariot piece is preserved in /blender/pieces/generate_rook_pandava.py in the repository structure above. It is identical to the v1 bible specification and is not rewritten here.

Cultural review and scriptwriting — AI agent led, human reviewer optional

Per the user's instruction, AI agents handle chapter scriptwriting (Claude or Gemini) and initial cultural review (the same agents with explicit prompts on tone, sensitivity, and source-material handling). The agents are explicitly told: write in original prose, never quote Gita verses, treat characters as game characters rather than religious figures, do not invent doctrinal positions.

Stage 1 contains only Vyasa and Yudhishthira dialogue plus the Chapter III Mirror reveal narration. Krishna does not appear until Stage 2 (Chapter XII). Before Krishna's script is locked in Stage 2, an honest review by 3–5 practicing Hindu chess players is non-negotiable — this is the one place the AI-led approach has a known failure mode that is too risky to ship past without human eyes.

Section Twelve

Acceptance Criteria and Honest Probabilities— what passes, what fails, what each outcome means.

Three stages, three probability assessments. The numbers are estimates based on the pressure tests' arguments, the prior art (Maia, Chess.com retention, indie chess product history), and the structural challenges identified in the design. They are presented honestly, not optimistically.

Multiplying through: the probability of the full seven-mode franchise reaching commercial success starting from the current state is around 1–3%. That is not zero, and it is not a reason to stop — many extraordinary products started at lower odds — but it is the right number to hold honestly. The reason to build this is not "this will probably succeed." It is: the artistic vision is real, the founder-market fit is genuine on the technical side, the cost of finding out is low (Stage 0 is a weekend and $0), and shipping a working indie game with serious ML behind it is a worthwhile outcome regardless of franchise odds.

What "success" honestly looks like at each stage

Stage 0 success

200–500 test sessions over two weeks. 30%+ unprompted "felt like me" feedback. 60%+ self-recognition rate. A short Twitter thread or r/chess post by a chess player at 1600+ rating that brings 50–200 organic visitors. A waitlist of 100–500 emails for Stage 1.

Stage 1 success

1,000–5,000 beta users over 8–10 weeks. 30%+ D1 retention. 10%+ complete all three chapters. ≥5 unprompted chess Twitter posts. One streamer or content creator covers the build. A waitlist of 1,000–5,000 for Stage 2.

Stage 2 success

Public launch. 10,000–100,000 lifetime players over the first year. Coverage in chess media (Chess.com news, ChessBase, GothamChess mention). If monetized: $20K–$200K Year 1 revenue. Strong enough signal to justify designing Mode 2.

What "failure" honestly looks like — and why it is not catastrophic

If Stage 0 fails, the entire project ends in three weeks with $0 spent and one shipped portfolio piece (the prototype itself, which demonstrates an end-to-end consumer ML product with chess engines, style vectors, and a self-recognition test — a genuinely strong technical resume addition).

If Stage 1 fails, the project ends in three months with under $200 spent and a shipped indie chess product on the open web. The product exists, the work is real, the experience is valuable. The seven-mode franchise does not happen; one polished web experiment does.

If Stage 2 fails at launch but ships, the work exists. Mode 1 lives on the open web for anyone to find. Players who do find it have a real experience. The artistic vision is honored even at small scale.
