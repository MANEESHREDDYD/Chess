The Vision

Chess has been the same game for fifteen hundred years. The board, the pieces, the rules — these are the most thoroughly tested system in human history. We change nothing about them. What we change is the company you keep while you play.

MIRROR is a chess game where you become an apprentice in the age of the Mahabharata. You travel from Hastinapura to Indraprastha to the forests of exile to the battlefield at Kurukshetra. You play Yudhishthira, who refuses to deceive you. You play Bhima, who laughs as he sacrifices his queen. You play Karna, who throws away his best move because honor will not let him take it. You learn from Drona, who teaches you the pieces the way he taught Arjuna. And at the end of every road, you meet the Mirror — an opponent built from the way you, specifically, play chess.

The product is free. It will remain free until it has earned the right to ask for anything. Chess.com has millions of users because it has been earning their trust for two decades. We will earn ours one game at a time, by being the first chess experience that makes a player say: "This thing understands me."

This document is the complete specification. It assumes you have read the strategic staging plan in v2 — Stage 0, Stage 1, Stage 2, behavioral gates, free-tier infrastructure, PWA-first. That document answers how we build. This one answers what we build, in detail enough that AI coding agents can write the code and an artist can render the world.

Section One

The WorldSetting, tone, sensory direction. What it feels like to be inside this game.

A great game is a place. Before we describe characters or chapters, we describe the air the player breathes when they open the app — the temperature, the colors, the sound of the wood when a piece is set down, the slant of light at four in the afternoon.

The age

The Mahabharata as a setting is not historical India and not mythic India — it is the India of an undated age before the iron of the Kuru war, where the Pandavas have just built their capital at Indraprastha and the wheel of dharma is turning toward Kurukshetra. The player arrives in this world as Vatsala, a young chaturanga apprentice from a village too small to have a name on any map. They carry nothing but a wooden board and a cloth roll of pieces.

The light is always one of three: warm oil-lamp gold for indoor scenes, the cool blue of dawn for matches with Arjuna, the orange dust of dusk for the battlefield chapters. There is no harsh midday sun in this game. We never see noon. The world feels perpetually on the edge of something — either becoming or ending.

The sound (deferred to Stage 2; described here so the future build understands the brief)

When music is added, it will be sparse. Indian classical raga, but only single instruments — sitar alone, sarangi alone, bansuri alone. No orchestral swells. The sound of a chess piece set on wood is the loudest sound in this game. Footsteps on stone. A door closing. A river. The wind in the forest of exile.

The space around the board

This is the design decision that separates MIRROR from every other chess product: the chess board is never the whole screen. The board occupies roughly sixty percent of the playable area. The remaining space shows where you are. When you play Yudhishthira, you see the pavilion at Indraprastha behind the board — open sides, white marble floor, attendants in the background. When you play Bhima, you see the practice yard, dust, weights, wrestling pits. When you play the Mirror, you see a riverbank at dusk and a figure that mirrors your every gesture.

This is not decoration. The setting tells the player who they are playing in a way that piece tunings cannot. A player who has fought Bhima will remember the smell of the wrestling yard. A player who has lost to Karna will remember the bowstring across his chest. The world is the storytelling.

The seven worlds

Mode 1 is one of seven planned story modes. Each future mode is a different world the apprentice could be sent to — a Cold War in 1973 where chess is espionage, a modern urban street where chess is hustle, a courtroom of cosmic gods, a sentient AI that learned chess from the player's own data. The framing that makes them feel like one game is that the same player carries the same Mirror across all seven worlds. Vyasa, the mentor, appears at the start of each new world as the player's guide. The throughline is the Mirror; the worlds are texture.

Mode 1 is what this document specifies. The other six are noted in v2 as a roadmap and not designed here.

Section Two

The Complete CastFifteen souls. Each with a visual identity, a chess signature, a voice. Designed in enough detail that an AI artist could render them and an AI writer could speak as them.

A character in this game is three things at once: a face the player learns to recognize, a way of playing chess that nobody else plays, and a voice that does not sound like any other voice. If a character cannot pass all three tests, they do not earn their chapter.

Mentor · Narrator
Vyasa
The compiler of the Mahabharata. He is writing the story as the player lives it. The player's matches are the source material.

Visual

Elderly male, late seventies in apparent age but with indeterminacy in the eyes that suggests longer. Height 168 cm, slightly stooped. Deep brown weathered skin, white beard reaching mid-chest, long white hair gathered in a loose top-knot. Eyes dark and sharp — the only feature on his face that does not look old.

Robes of saffron-ochre cotton (hex #c87533), draped over the left shoulder. Wrapped prayer beads at the wrist. Ink-stained fingers on the right hand. A pale scar across the left palm. Always seated cross-legged on a low wooden platform. Oil lamp at his side. Stack of palm-leaf manuscripts. A single peacock feather marking his current page.

Lighting: oil-lamp warm (#f4a460) from below-side, casting long shadows behind him. Never in full daylight in Mode 1.

Where he appears

Calibration (Chapter I). Beginning and end of every story chapter as the framing voice. The post-game review across all chapters and beyond.

Chess signature

Plays Maia 1500 with no constraints, only during the calibration match. He is the system after that — every post-game analysis appears as his commentary. He has memory across all games and refers backward.

Voice

Patient, slightly weary, sometimes amused. Speaks in measured pace, often pauses mid-sentence. Never raises his voice. Uses old-fashioned phrasing.

"Move twelve. The same square you lost the knight to Bhima three days ago. The lesson does not stick."
"You are not what I thought you were. That is good news. I thought wrongly."

Animation philosophy

Minimal. A slight turn of the head when speaking. A single hand gesture per exchange, never more. The oil lamp flickers; he does not.

Kuru Minister · First Opponent · Chapter II
Vidura
The wise minister of Hastinapura. The first real opponent — gentle on purpose, but never patronizing.

Visual

Older male, sixty-ish, slender frame. Height 172 cm. Light brown skin (#9a7050), short white-grey beard, balding head. Wears plain off-white robes with a single thin sash of Pandava indigo. A scroll tucked into his belt. Eyes intelligent, kind, missing nothing.

Setting (Chapter II): a small library room in Hastinapura. Shelves of palm-leaf books rising on three sides. A single low table with the chessboard. Late morning light through a high window. One cushion for the player, one for him.

Where he appears

Chapter II only in Mode 1. Reappears in Stage 2 in the dice game chapter as a silent witness to Yudhishthira's ruin.

Chess signature

Maia 1500 with a teaching bias — in positions where multiple reasonable moves exist, prefers the one that creates the most instructive position rather than the strongest. He is designed to lose to most players if the player is careful, and to win against careless play. He is the audience-on-ramp.

Voice

Warm, articulate, talks throughout the match. Explains his thinking aloud, even at his own expense.

"I should not have moved the bishop there. I know this. And yet — would you like to see what I was thinking?"
Pandava · King · Chapter III
Yudhishthira
The eldest Pandava. The king who refuses to bluff. The player will lose this match more often than they win, and that is the point.

Visual

Tall, 180 cm, slender, dignified bearing. Mid-thirties. Calm face, sad eyes that look slightly past the viewer. Light brown skin (#a87852), dark trimmed beard, long dark hair tied back at the nape.

White-and-gold silks with a single sash in Pandava indigo (#1e3a5f) crossing the chest. Gold armbands. A simple gold circlet, not a full crown — he is a king who does not insist on being seen as one. The chessboard sits between him and the player on a low marble table.

Setting: a pavilion in Indraprastha. White marble floor, open sides showing gardens, attendants in the soft background. Afternoon light, golden but not harsh.

Chess signature

Maia 1700 + no-swindle constraint. In positions where Stockfish identifies a tactical move whose success requires the opponent to fail at depth-6 calculation, Yudhishthira selects the second-best evaluation. He refuses to win by trickery. He plays the true position. He is harder than his rating suggests because most players win blitz chess on tricks, and tricks do not work on him.

Openings: Caro-Kann, Slav Defense, Queen's Pawn Game, London System. Never gambits.

Voice

Measured, formal, occasionally sad. Speaks in complete sentences with subordinate clauses. Concedes losses without protest. Wins without satisfaction.

"That move is not wrong. It is, however, beneath what the position offers. Look again."
"You won. I am glad. I will need to think tonight about how."
Pandava · Wrestler · Chapter IV
Bhima
The strongman. The middle Pandava brother. He loves attacking chess the way he loves wrestling — for the contact, for the noise, for the moment of contact when both bodies meet.

Visual

Massive, 195 cm, exaggeratedly muscular but not bodybuilder-defined — a wrestler's body, thick at chest and shoulders. Mid-thirties. Deep brown skin (#6b4226). Heavy black beard, thick mustache, heavy brows. Wide grin frequently visible — Bhima laughs often, at himself and at others.

Bare chest. Dhoti in bronze and indigo. A leather wrestler's belt at the waist. Bronze armbands. A mace propped against his chair, never used during matches but always present.

Setting: the practice yard at Indraprastha. Dust, weights, wrestling pits. Late morning bright sun (the only fully-lit scene in Mode 1 — intentional). Bhima is seated on a stool that is visibly too small for him, knees high, leaning forward over the board.

Chess signature

Maia 1500 + attacking bias. In evaluation, capturing moves and moves that attack opponent pieces receive a +30 centipawn weight. Trades are accepted aggressively. Defensive consolidation moves are deprioritized.

Openings: King's Gambit, Sicilian Najdorf, King's Indian Attack, Center Game.

Weakness: patient defense and counterattack. Overextends.

Voice

Loud, joyful, easily distracted. Roars when he likes a move — his own or the opponent's.

"YES! Trade your queen! Trade everything! Let us see what is left when the smoke clears!"
"You won? Good. Now we wrestle. I am joking. Mostly."
Pandava · Archer · Chapter V
Arjuna
The precise one. The greatest archer of the age. He plays the best move every time. There is no flourish. He is the wall the player must scale.

Visual

Slender, athletic, 178 cm. Late twenties. Quietly beautiful in a hard, focused way. Light brown skin (#a07550). Clean-shaven. Sharp jaw. Dark eyes that focus on the board as if aiming. Long dark hair in a single tight braid.

Hunter's white tunic with deep indigo trim. Leather quiver and bow at his back — set aside during matches but visible. A single silver earring.

Setting: the archery field at dawn. Long shadows, mist on the ground. Wooden targets in the background. Cool grey-blue light. Arjuna is seated cross-legged on the ground. The board sits on a low flat stone between them. His posture is impeccable. Hands rest on knees between moves. The bow visible in the background should be the most beautiful object in the scene.

Chess signature

Maia 1900 + low policy temperature (0.6). He plays the top engine move in most positions. He does not vary for variety's sake.

Openings: Catalan, Berlin Defense, Nimzo-Indian, Petroff. Theoretically dense, principled.

Weakness: early middlegame complexity. Positions where calculation gets fuzzy.

Voice

Spare. Direct. No wasted words. Will not engage in small talk. Acknowledges good play with a single nod.

"That move loses a tempo. Continue."
"You played the position. That is enough."
Pandava Twins · Chapter XVII (2v2 unlock)
Nakula & Sahadeva
The Pandava twins. They never appear alone in Mode 1 — only as a pair, only in the 2v2 chapter.

Visual

Identical twin frames, late twenties, 175 cm each. Nakula is the talkative one with an expressive face. Sahadeva is silent and serious. Both wear matching white-and-indigo tunics. Distinguishable only by expression and a single accessory difference: Nakula wears a silver earring on the right ear, Sahadeva on the left.

Setting: a courtyard at twilight. Two chessboards side by side on a long low table. Lanterns floating just above the boards.

Chess signature

Maia 1700 each, with a shared utility function. In 2v2 mode (bughouse variant with strict chess rules, captured pieces passable between boards), they preferentially pass useful captured pieces between boards. The coordinated AI is what makes them harder than the sum of two individual 1700 opponents.

Voice

Nakula speaks; Sahadeva does not. Sahadeva nods or shakes his head.

Nakula: "He needs a knight. Sahadeva — pass him a knight." (Sahadeva nods slowly without looking up.)

Unlock

Defeating the twins in Chapter XVII unlocks the 2v2 multiplayer mode for use in the wider game.

Loyal to Kauravas · Tragic · Chapters IX and XVI
Karna
The tragic one. A great player who undercuts himself out of loyalty to a man he does not respect. The most heartbreaking opponent in Mode 1.

Visual

Powerful, athletic, 183 cm. Late twenties. Carries a melancholy that does not lift. Bronze-brown skin (#8a5530). Beard trimmed close. Hair worn loose, falling to the shoulders. Eyes downcast often.

Gold-plated armor over a dark crimson tunic — the gold armor is dented, scratched, well-used. A single bowstring across his chest. A diagonal scar across his left cheek.

Setting (Chapter IX): a forest clearing at midday. Dappled sunlight. Hunting kit beside him. Setting (Chapter XVI): a battlefield at twilight. Dust, distant cries, broken chariots in the background.

Chess signature

Maia 1700 + loyalty constraint. In positions where the best move requires a tactic Karna considers dishonorable — a deliberate provocation to force a fork, a king-hunt initiated by a sacrifice the opponent did not foresee — Karna selects the second-best move. Implementation: a small classifier trained on annotated honorable-vs-exploitative moves applied as a filter.

Openings: Romantic-era. Ruy Lopez, Italian, Two Knights Defense.

Notable: often plays a worse move that looks correct by classical principles. A player who beats Karna learns to recognize when classical instincts are wrong.

Voice

Quiet, formal, sad. Speaks of duty often.

"There is a better move. I will not play it. Do you know why?"
"You won. I should have won. I chose not to. That is the difference between us."
Kaurava · Crown Prince · Chapter XI
Duryodhana
The ambitious cousin. He will not lose what he has. He will not stop wanting more.

Visual

Tall, powerful, intimidating. 190 cm, early thirties. Carries his body like someone used to being obeyed. Brown skin (#7a5238). Thick beard kept sharp at the edges. Eyes that glare without blinking. Heavy gold earrings.

Ornate crimson silks with black-gold embroidery (Kaurava palette: #8b2635 with #1c1814 obsidian and gold thread). Heavy gold pectoral. A full crown, unlike Yudhishthira's circlet.

Setting (Chapter XI): the throne hall of Hastinapura. Dark marble floor. Torchlight. Court figures watching from the wings. A subtle threat in the air. Duryodhana on a throne-like chair, leaning back, right hand on the armrest, left hand holding a cup of wine. He leans forward reluctantly to make each move, as if it costs him dignity.

Chess signature

Maia 1700 + material accumulation bias. Gaining material is weighted +40 centipawns. Trading equal material is weighted -20 cp. Captures pieces aggressively even when it costs position. Hates to exchange queens.

Openings: sharp, aggressive. Sicilian (as black), English (as white), Grünfeld.

Weakness: sacrifice. He takes material that exposes his king. Beat him by offering pieces.

Voice

Imperious, suspicious, mocking. Threatens the player obliquely during the match.

"You move quickly. Either you are confident, or you are stupid. I will know which by move twenty."
Kaurava · Optional · Chapter XI side encounter
Dushasana
Duryodhana's brother. Cruel, swindling. A brief encounter, not a major arc.

Visual

Tall, heavyset. Crimson robes. Coarse beard. A perpetual sneer. Setting: a wine-room adjacent to Duryodhana's throne hall, torchlight, low ceiling.

Chess signature

Maia 1500 + cheap-tactic bias. Weights moves that threaten one-move tactical wins — Scholar's Mate patterns, basic forks. Falls apart in slow positions.

"You think too much. Just move. Just move. Just move."
Kaurava · The Schemer · Chapters VI and XVIII
Shakuni
The master of games. Duryodhana's uncle. He set the trap that destroyed the Pandavas at dice. He is the final opponent before the Mirror.

Visual

Old, lean, slightly twisted. 165 cm, sixties, walks with a limp. Pale brown skin (#7c5238) with sun-spots. Sharp narrow eyes that miss nothing and reveal less. Thin grey beard. Hair pulled back, oiled.

Dark crimson and indigo robes — Kaurava palette but more elaborate than Duryodhana's. Many small gold ornaments. Rings on every finger. Three loaded dice in his left hand, always. Even at the chess table, the dice are in his palm. He rolls them softly between his fingers.

Setting (Chapter VI): the dice hall of Hastinapura, heavy oil lamps, many witnesses. Setting (Chapter XVIII): an underground chamber lit by a single oil lamp. The chamber must feel claustrophobic — the player should sense the walls.

Chess signature

Maia 1900 + adversarial trap mode. Shakuni does not play the best move against the position — he plays the move most likely to induce error against the specific player. Implementation: train Shakuni's move policy on a dataset where each position is paired with the move that maximized opponent blunders in historical games at the player's Elo level.

Openings: tricky systems. Trompowsky, Veresov, Stafford Gambit traps as black, Halloween Gambit.

Notable: the hardest opponent match in Mode 1. Players who underestimate Shakuni after beating Bhishma are walking into exactly the mistake the design intends.

Voice

Soft-spoken, falsely warm. Calls the player "child" regardless of age. Smiles often. The dice rolling between his fingers should be visible in every shot.

"Take the pawn. It is free. Surely you can see that it is free."
"Did you think this match was about chess? It was always about you."
Grandsire · Loyal to Throne · Chapter X
Bhishma
The grandsire. Bound by an old vow to defend whoever sits on the throne. The fortress the player must grind down.

Visual

Ancient but not stooped. Powerful frame eroded by age. 188 cm, apparent age 80+ but indeterminate — he has lived too long. Lined weathered skin (#7a5640). Vast white beard. White hair. Eyes pale grey, unfocused at rest, sharp when engaged.

Worn battle armor under a white shroud. The armor is unornate, functional, scratched, real. A simple white cloth wraps his shoulders.

Setting: an old stone fortification at the edge of Hastinapura. Late afternoon. Wind carrying dust. A single weathered banner of the Kuru house. Bhishma seated on a low stone bench, back perfectly straight despite age. Hands folded.

Chess signature

Maia 1900 + fortress bias. King safety weighted +60 cp. Passed pawns on his side are protected. He refuses to open his own position. Maximum defensive calculation depth extended to 22 plies.

Openings: closed systems. French Tarrasch, Cambridge Springs, Stonewall Attack, Slav.

Weakness: time. He must be ground down. The match is designed to push past 60 moves — short games against Bhishma are unwinnable.

Voice

Slow, deliberate, generous with praise but spare with it. The most likely opponent to compliment the player after a loss.

"You have not lost yet. Most do, by now."
"That move was true. I know what is coming. I cannot stop it. Continue."
Teacher · Loyal to Throne · Chapters VIII (Coach) and XV (story match)
Drona
The teacher. Master of arms. He runs the Gurukul — the training mode is, in narrative, his school. He is also the voice of the Coach.

Visual

Lean, ascetic, 175 cm. Late fifties. Stern bearing. Light brown skin (#9a7050). Short white beard. Shaved head with a small tuft at the crown (the brahmin shikha). Eyes dark, intense.

Plain white dhoti and shawl. A single sacred thread across the torso left-to-right. No ornaments. A wooden teaching staff.

Setting (Chapter VIII): a forest grove with students' practice gear scattered. Morning light filtered through trees. Drona standing during lessons, seated only when playing matches. Hands often behind his back when speaking.

Chess signature

Maia 1800 in training drills, scaled to player Elo + 100. In the story match (Chapter XV), plays at his actual strength — the player is not expected to win. It is the "humility before mastery" chapter.

Openings: classical. Italian, Ruy Lopez, Queen's Gambit Declined.

Voice

Severe but fair. Speaks in lessons even outside training. The Coach voice for the entire post-game analysis system in the Mahabharata theme — see §5.

"You have learned the bishop. You have not learned to feel where the bishop wants to go. That is the next year of work."
Strategist · Chapter XII
Krishna
A strategist who has advised both sides. He appears once in Mode 1. Treated within the game as a character — not as a deity. No quoted Gita material.

Visual

Medium height, athletic, 175 cm. Mid-thirties. Calm bearing. The iconic blue skin tone preserved as character visual identity (deep indigo dye, #3a4a78 — not bright cartoon blue). Dark hair with a single peacock feather woven in. Calm eyes.

Yellow-gold dhoti. Garland of forest flowers around the neck. Bare chest. A single conch shell at his waist. No royal regalia. The flower garland and feather should be the most intricate visual elements on him.

Setting: a riverbank at night. Stars. Cooking fire low. A war camp in the middle distance. Krishna seated cross-legged across from the player. The board between them is small and simple, not a royal board. The intimacy is the point.

Chess role

Not playable as an opponent. Chapter XII is structured as a single chess match between the player and a "shadow opponent" (in narrative, the war itself — mechanically, Maia 1800). Between each of the player's moves, Krishna speaks. His dialogue is original, addressing themes of action, focus, and outcome.

The player can request a hint from Krishna up to three times in the chapter. Krishna's hint is not a move suggestion — it is a frame for how to approach the position. Implementation: a curated tree of 20 short hints triggered by position type.

Critical writing rule

Original prose only. No Gita quotation. No paraphrase close enough to be substitutive. The Stage 2 build will engage 3-5 practicing Hindu chess players for a sensitivity review before script lockdown — non-negotiable. AI agents draft the script; humans verify before release.

Queen of the Pandavas · Side content
Draupadi
Queen, observer, vowed to vengeance. Does not play chess directly — gives the player challenge matches in optional side content.

Visual

Tall, strong, dark-haired. Late twenties. Deep red and gold royal silks. A single garland of jasmine. Eyes that hold judgment. She is always shown standing, never seated. The player looks up at her.

The Final Opponent · Chapters VII, XIV, XIX
The Mirror
The player's reflection. An AI model trained on the way the player plays. The throughline of the entire seven-mode game.

Visual

A faceless figure rendered in the same proportions and silhouette as the player's avatar. The pieces it plays with are the player's faction (Pandava blue and gold), but rendered in inverted lighting — as if the same scene lit from a different sun.

Setting (Chapter VII): a riverbank at dusk. Mist on the water. A small fire between the two boards. Setting (Chapter XIV): a battlefield at dusk. Smoke instead of mist. A torch instead of a fire. Setting (Chapter XIX): a space that is none of the above — a black void with only the chessboard lit. The Final Mirror is the player. The space confirms it.

Animation: when the Mirror moves a piece, the player's avatar (in the cinematic frame, not the live board) makes the same gesture. The Mirror is the player. The visual is unsettling because it is true.

Chess signature

See §4 for the full Mirror spec. Summary: a three-layer hybrid. Base layer is Maia tuned to the player's Elo. Personalization layer reranks Maia's move policy using the player's eight-dimension style vector. Exploitation layer switches to a perturbed Stockfish line in approximately 20% of positions where the player's weak motifs can be tested.

Three appearances, each with deeper data. By Chapter XIX, the Mirror is trained on 60+ matches plus calibration.

Voice

The Mirror never speaks. Vyasa narrates each encounter. The Mirror only plays.

The failure mode

The Mirror must feel like the player. If the first Mirror feels like a generic Stockfish in costume, the entire game's central promise fails. This is what Stage 0 in v2 tests before anything else gets built.

Section Three

The Story in FullNineteen chapters. Three acts. Twelve to fifteen hours for a complete playthrough. Written here in narrative form, not as bullet points — because a story bible without prose is a feature list.

What follows is the script in shape, not in final line-by-line dialogue. The full dialogue is written by AI agents in Stage 2 using these chapter descriptions as briefs, then reviewed by the founder for tonal consistency, then put in front of a small cohort of beta readers — chess players and Mahabharata-literate readers in equal measure — before release.

Act I — The Apprentice's Awakening

Six chapters. The player arrives in the world, learns its rules, meets the three eldest Pandavas, and witnesses the dice game that breaks everything. The act ends with the Pandavas exiled and the player at a crossroads.

Chapter I · Calibration · 15–18 min · Vyasa
The Test by the Banyan Tree
In which an old sage finds an apprentice he was not looking for.

The game opens on Vatsala, the player's character, in a small village at the edge of the Vatsala forest. They are alone with a wooden chessboard on a low stone, working through a position by themselves. A traveler stops to watch. The traveler is old, dressed in saffron, carrying nothing but a walking stick and a satchel of palm-leaf manuscripts. He sits down across from the player without asking.

"Show me how you play," he says. The calibration begins.

Eight tasks, framed as Vyasa's examination of the apprentice — a tactical puzzle in which the player chooses among three candidate moves, an opening choice with six possibilities, an endgame to solve, a tactical race, a moral chess position, a repertoire prompt, and one short game against Vyasa himself. Each task is preceded by one or two lines of his dialogue establishing what the task reveals. At the end he closes his manuscript and stands.

"You are not what I expected," he says. "I expected nothing in particular. So that is consistent. Come with me. There is something I want you to see before the war begins."

MechanicCalibration logged. Style vector computed and persisted. The Mirror begins training the moment the player makes their first move.
Chapter II · Story · 25 min · Vidura
Hastinapura
In which the apprentice sees the great city for the first time and learns what is at stake.

Vyasa brings the player into Hastinapura through the eastern gate at dawn. The city is rich and tense. Banners of the Kuru house hang from every parapet. Vyasa walks the player through the palace, past the throne where Bhishma sits silently, past the courtyard where Drona is teaching a group of younger boys, past a high balcony where Duryodhana is watching them without expression.

They arrive at Vidura's library. Vidura is the wise minister of the Kuru court — a brother of Bhishma in spirit if not in blood. He has been told, somehow, that the player is coming. He sets up a board and begins to speak.

The match is the tutorial match. Vidura plays gentle chess on purpose and talks the player through it. He explains the political situation as the pieces move. Two cousins. Two claims to the throne. One inheritance that was promised and then taken back. A king who is blind in both eyes (Dhritarashtra) and a queen who chose to blindfold herself in solidarity (Gandhari). The Pandavas have a new capital at Indraprastha because the elders gave them half a kingdom to keep the peace. The Kauravas hate this arrangement. The dice game is coming.

"You will play all of them before you leave," Vidura says, taking a piece carelessly that the player could have defended. "I hope you remember me as the kind one. I am the only kind one in the family."

MechanicStandard match, Maia 1500. Most players win. The first match a player likely wins becomes a confidence anchor.
Chapter III · Story · 30 min · Yudhishthira
Indraprastha
In which the apprentice meets a king who refuses to trick him, and learns what that costs.

Vyasa and the player travel south. Two days on foot. The new Pandava capital at Indraprastha rises out of the plain like something built yesterday because it was. The palace gates are open. The streets are full of artisans putting the finishing touches on a city that has not yet decided what it wants to be.

Yudhishthira receives them in a pavilion of white marble. He is younger than the player expected — only mid-thirties — and quieter. He pours water for the player himself. Then he gestures at the board.

"My uncle Vidura said you would come. He said you played well. I would like to see for myself."

The match begins. Yudhishthira plays a calm, principled chess. The player tries the kinds of tactical tricks that worked against Vidura. None of them work. Yudhishthira sees every threat and refuses to fall for any of them. Section Four

The MirrorThe mechanic that defines the game. Engineered honestly, including its known limits and the countermeasures designed to address them.

A perturbed Stockfish weighted by sixteen dimensions creates uncanny chess. A pure Maia plays human but does not feel like any specific human. The Mirror is neither — it is a three-layer hybrid designed against the two known failure modes pressure tests have flagged.

The three-layer architecture

Layer 1 — Maia base

The foundation. The Mirror starts from Maia tuned to the player's calibration Elo (Maia 1100, 1300, 1500, 1700, or 1900 depending on detected band). Maia is published by CSSLab and trained to predict human moves. This guarantees that, at baseline, the Mirror plays moves a human at the player's level would actually consider. No uncanny valley at this layer.

Layer 2 — Style-vector reranking

Maia returns a probability distribution over candidate moves. The personalization layer reranks that distribution using the player's eight-dimension style vector — boosting moves that match the player's opening repertoire and tactical preferences, demoting moves the player would not consider. This is the layer that makes the Mirror feel personal.

Layer 3 — Opportunistic Stockfish exploitation

In roughly 20% of positions — specifically, positions where the player has a known weak tactical motif (fork, pin, skewer, removing-the-defender) — the Mirror switches to a perturbed Stockfish line that creates exactly the position the player tends to misjudge. This is the layer that makes the Mirror feel like it is trying to test the player.

The eight style-vector dimensions (Stage 1 — Stage 2 expands to sixteen)

  1. Opening repertoire (top 3 White, top 3 Black)
  2. Average move time
  3. Time-pressure blunder rate (moves with under 5s clock that lose ≥150 cp)
  4. Exchange willingness (proportion of equal trades accepted)
  5. Preferred minor piece (knight-keeper vs bishop-keeper)
  6. Tactical motif blindness (miss rate on forks, pins, skewers from calibration)
  7. Endgame technique (win rate from K+R, K+Q advantage in calibration)
  8. Pawn-structure preference (open vs closed positions)

The tunable Mirror

After the first Mirror match, the player sees their style vector visualized as a radar with plain-English axis labels. "You play attacking openings. You take time on every move. You miss forks more than pins." The player can drag any axis up or down to disagree. The Mirror regenerates immediately and offers a second match. This is the answer to the low-Elo signal problem — the data alone cannot always distinguish two 1200-rated players, but the player's own self-knowledge can.

Self-recognition test — the only behavioral check that matters

After the first Mirror match, the player sees three unlabeled style profiles: their own Mirror, a generic Maia at their Elo, and a different player's Mirror from the cohort. The player is asked "which one was you." Chance baseline is 33%. Acceptance threshold is 60%. This is the cleanest measurement of whether the Mirror is meaningfully personalized.

Section Five

The CoachWhy players will return tomorrow. The retention mechanic that pressure tests correctly identified as the missing piece in the previous documents.

If the Mirror is the hook — the reason the game gets shared — the Coach is the reason the game gets opened again. Mirror is novelty without Coach. Mirror plus Coach is a daily product.

What the Coach does

After every game — story, Mirror, multiplayer, free practice — Drona appears (in Mahabharata theme; a neutral "Coach" character in Standard theme) and selects one specific moment from the game to discuss. The moment is chosen by Stockfish's analysis: the highest centipawn-loss blunder or the largest missed brilliancy.

The Coach does five things:

  1. Calls out one specific moment. Not the whole game. Not a summary. One move, named, in character voice.
  2. Tags the tactical motif. Fork, pin, skewer, hanging piece, missed defensive resource. Tagged via a small tactical classifier running over the game's Stockfish analysis.
  3. Tracks the pattern over time. If the player has missed forks five times in their last ten games, the Coach says so. "This is the fifth time. Sit. We will fix this now."
  4. Serves personalized puzzles. A drill of 3-5 puzzles targeting that specific motif, drawn from the Lichess open puzzle database (CC0, 4 million+ puzzles).
  5. Adapts difficulty. The player's accuracy on each motif is tracked. Mastered motifs are deprioritized. New weaknesses are picked up.

A sample Coach interaction

After Chapter III · Loss to Yudhishthira · Coach: Drona
"Move sixteen. You moved the knight from f3 to d4. The knight was attacked by Yudhishthira's bishop, but the bishop was also defending his queen. You did not see that your own bishop on g5 could have captured the queen instead. This is a fork pattern. It is the third time in your last ten games. We will work on this now."

[Five fork puzzles offered. Each ~30 seconds. Drona returns at the end with feedback. The whole intervention is six to eight minutes.]

Why the Coach is the answer to the pressure tests

Three pressure tests said: the Mirror is novelty, not habit. They were right. A player who plays the Mirror once and never returns has not learned anything; they have seen a parlor trick. The Coach turns this into a real loop:

  1. The Mirror makes the player lose.
  2. The Coach explains exactly why and offers to help.
  3. The player improves on a specific motif.
  4. The player plays the Mirror again, sharper.
  5. The Mirror has also improved (more data). The cycle continues.

This is the same loop that powers Chess.com's lessons feature and Duolingo's spaced-repetition language model. We are not inventing a category. We are bringing the loop to a product that also has a story.

Implementation

Analysis engine
Stockfish at depth 14 over every move in every game. Classification by standard centipawn-loss thresholds (>50 cp inaccuracy, >150 cp mistake, >300 cp blunder).
Motif classifier
Lightweight pattern detector. For each blunder, tag the motif. Open-source detectors available; we extend if needed.
Coach voice generation
Google Gemini 1.5 Flash via free API (1,500 requests/day, 15 RPM at Stage 1 scale this is comfortably under limit). Prompt engineered against Drona character profile. One call per Coach intervention.
Puzzle bank
Lichess open puzzle database (CC0). 4M+ puzzles filtered by motif tag, rating, theme. No proprietary content needed.
Personal weakness model
Local SQLite synced to Supabase. Tracks the player's history of motif failures with rolling counts and decay.
Daily limit
One Coach intervention per game. Capped at 5 Coach sessions per day on free tier. Soft prompt: "Drona suggests rest. Come back tomorrow."

The Daily Mirror — the habit loop

After the player completes Chapter VII (the first Mirror reveal), a new feature unlocks: the Daily Mirror. Once every 24 hours, the player can play one match against their current Mirror. The Mirror updates daily based on the previous day's games. Win streak tracked. Skipping a day does not break the streak; losing does.

This is the Wordle pattern applied to chess. One match per day. Ten to fifteen minutes. Free, no friction, no signup beyond the original session. The Daily Mirror is the answer to "would they return within 24 hours" — the pressure test's actual ask. If the Daily Mirror works, MIRROR is a product. If it does not, MIRROR is an art piece. Either is acceptable. Only one is a product.

Section Six

For Beginners and Masters AlikeOne game, two doors. The same content serves a new chess player learning the rules and a 1900 player exploring their own style — but the doors they enter through are different.

Chess.com tried to serve everyone with a single interface and ended up serving experts well and beginners poorly. Lichess tried to serve everyone with a minimalist interface and ended up serving experts well and beginners poorly. We will try a third thing: detect the level on entry and route accordingly.

  4. Our own application code is under whatever license we choose. The aggregation pattern does not impose GPL on it.

Sunday afternoon — the Mirror generator skeleton

src/engine/mirror.ts 
import { StyleVector } from "../ml/styleVector";

// Layer 1: Maia base. Layer 2: style reranking. Layer 3: opportunistic Stockfish.

export async function getMirrorMove(
  fen: string,
  styleVector: StyleVector,
  opponentElo: number
): Promise<string> {
  // 1. Detect if this position is one where the player has a known weakness
  const motifPresent = detectMotifInPosition(fen, styleVector);

  if (motifPresent && Math.random() < 0.2) {
    // Layer 3: switch to perturbed Stockfish to set up the weakness exploitation
    return await getPerturbedStockfishMove(fen, styleVector, opponentElo);
  }

  // Layer 1: Maia returns move probability distribution
  const maiaPolicy = await getMaiaPolicy(fen, opponentElo);

  // Layer 2: rerank Maia's policy using style vector
  const rerankedPolicy = rerankByStyle(maiaPolicy, styleVector, fen);

  // Sample from the reranked policy
  return sampleFromPolicy(rerankedPolicy);
}

function rerankByStyle(
  policy: { move: string; prob: number }[],
  vec: StyleVector,
  fen: string
): { move: string; prob: number }[] {
  return policy.map((entry) => {
    let bonus = 1.0;
    // If we're in the opening, prefer the player's repertoire
    if (isOpeningPhase(fen)) {
      if (vec.opening_white_top3.includes(entry.move)) bonus *= 1.4;
    }
    // If this move accepts an exchange and player tends to accept exchanges
    if (isExchangeMove(entry.move, fen) && vec.exchange_willingness > 0.6) {
      bonus *= 1.2;
    }
    return { move: entry.move, prob: entry.prob * bonus };
  });
}

Section Ten

How a Free Game WinsAgainst Chess.com's millions, against Lichess's network effects, against TikTok for the player's attention. The honest distribution strategy for a free product.

Free games that reach scale share a pattern. Wordle started as one engineer's gift to his partner and ended as a New York Times acquisition. Lichess has 4 million monthly users on zero advertising. Among Us was abandoned for years before TikTok rediscovered it. None of them charged on launch. All of them had one thing nobody else had.

The five things free games that scale share

1 · A one-sentence pitch a stranger can repeat

Wordle: "Guess the word in six tries." Among Us: "One of you is the imposter." Wordle scaled because the pitch fits in a tweet, and the tweet fits a screenshot of the colored squares. MIRROR's pitch: "A chess game where the final opponent is built from how you play." Eleven words. Repeatable. Generates curiosity in chess players and non-players alike.

2 · A shareable artifact from a single play session

Wordle's shareable artifact is the colored grid. Among Us's is the kill screen. MIRROR's is the post-Mirror match result card — a screenshot showing your Mirror's final position, your style vector visualized, and a one-line summary. "I lost to my own Mirror in 34 moves. It knew I always blunder in time pressure." The card is built to share. It is generated in Stage 0 as a required output.

3 · A daily reason to return

Wordle has a new puzzle every day. MIRROR has the Daily Mirror — one match per day against an opponent that gets sharper as the player improves. The Wordle of chess. Free, frictionless, ten to fifteen minutes, builds a habit through scarcity rather than feature volume.

4 · A creator distribution path

Among Us scaled because xQc, Pokimane, and Sykkuno started streaming it. MIRROR's creator path is chess-specific: GothamChess (7.5M subscribers), Botez sisters, smaller chess streamers in the 50K–500K range. The pitch to them is simple: "Play your Mirror on camera. Show your audience whether it knew you." A high-rated chess streamer playing their Mirror and reacting is the single most viral asset this game can produce. Cost: free, if the product is good enough. The product has to be good enough.

5 · A community that wants to defend it

Lichess is donation-funded because its community wants it to exist. r/chess defends Lichess against Chess.com without being asked. The MIRROR community we want is: chess players who like indie products, Mahabharata-literate readers who like chess, ML-curious players who want to see what their style vector looks like. We earn this community by treating them as collaborators — open source the chess code, publish the style vector methodology, let people self-host if they want. The community is the moat.
