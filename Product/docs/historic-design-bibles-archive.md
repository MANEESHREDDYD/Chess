color: var(--ink); } Design and Build Plan · v2 · Supersedes v1 MIRROR A Staged MVP A chess game where the final opponent is built from the way you play. Built as a PWA, validated in stages, on free tiers, with AI agents. Mode One of Seven Maneesh Reddy Duddukunta May 2026 Three pressure tests on the previous design bible reached the same conclusion: the document specified a twelve-week production for an assumption that has not been tested once. This document responds by re-architecting the entire build as three staged gates, where Stage 0 is a weekend prototype with a single job — verify whether the Mirror feels like the player. Everything that follows is contingent on that verdict. What the three pressure tests collectively said The Mirror may not be magical. Maia already provides human-like chess. A perturbed Stockfish weighted by a sixteen-dimensional style vector may produce an opponent that oscillates between brilliance and blunder rather than one that feels like the player. The hook of the entire seven-mode franchise rests on this working. At low Elo, style vectors converge. Players rated 1000–1500 — eighty percent of the addressable market — blunder so much that their style data looks similar. The Mirror may be distinguishable only for players above 1800, who are not the commercial market. Scope is overbuilt. Nineteen chapters, fifteen characters, Blender procedural pipeline, multiplayer, analysis suite, three platforms — designed before any evidence that the central mechanic resonates. The hook is buried. The first Mirror reveal at Chapter VII sits behind three to four hours of story. The chess audience has a median session length of twelve minutes. Most users will churn before they ever see the product's central promise. The Mahabharata framing narrows the market. Resonant for South Asian players (lower willingness to pay) and friction for Western players (higher willingness to pay). The framing must be optional, not mandatory. What this document changes Three stages, two kill criteria. Stage 0 is a weekend prototype. If it fails, the project ends. Stage 1 is a ten-week thin slice with a Beta cohort and behavioral acceptance criteria. If it fails, no Stage 2. Stage 2 is the full Mode 1 — only if Stage 1 evidence justifies it. PWA-first, not native. Single web codebase served by Cloudflare Pages free tier. Works on iOS, Android, Windows, and desktop browsers. No app store fees, no native build complexity, faster iteration. The Mirror is surfaced early. The first Mirror reveal moves from Chapter VII to Chapter III, within the first ninety minutes of play. The Mahabharata framing becomes an optional theme toggle. Default piece set is standard chess; Mahabharata is a skin. The Mirror is tunable. After the first Mirror match, the player is asked a single direct question — "did that play like you?" — and can adjust the style vector by reweighting dimensions. This addresses the low-Elo signal problem by letting the player co-create their reflection rather than receive it. A second AI agent: the Coach. Separate from the Mirror. The Coach is a teaching agent that engages every player after every game, identifies their weakest tactical motif, and serves personalized puzzles in a character voice. Powered by Gemini's free tier. Serves the requirement that the product help beginners learn and skilled players improve. Free-tier infrastructure throughout. Cloudflare Pages (frontend hosting, free unlimited bandwidth), Supabase free tier (database, auth, realtime, 50K MAU ceiling), Stockfish WASM (client-side, free), Maia weights (open source via lc0 WASM), Gemini API free tier (1,500 requests per day for Coach text generation), Blender Studio (free for asset generation). Total recurring infrastructure cost at MVP: $0. GPL compliance baked in. Stockfish and Maia are GPLv3. The PWA bundles them as separate WASM modules called via interface, distributes them with proper notices, links to source. The application code remains under the user's chosen license. A weekend prototype gates a ten-week build, which gates the complete Mode 1 release. Each stage has a single primary question, a behavioral pass criterion, and a kill criterion that is honored if the data says so. Stage 0 Mirror Prototype 2 weekends · $0 Stage 1 Hook Test 8–10 weeks · &lt;$200 Stage 2 Complete Mode 1 10–14 weeks · &lt;$2,000 Why staging is non-negotiable A solo developer with AI agents and a free-tier stack can ship surprising amounts of code. That changes the build economics but not the product economics. The Mirror is the product. If it does not feel like the player to a meaningful fraction of users, no amount of art, story, character work, or analysis tooling redeems the central claim of the game. Three independent pressure tests converged on this. The honest response is to build the smallest possible test of that claim before building anything else. The decision points Decision · After Stage 0 · 2-3 weeks in Does the Mirror feel like the player? Measured by: ≥30% of test users describe the Mirror as "playing like me" unprompted in feedback, AND the per-user Mirror passes a self-recognition check (player picks "my Mirror" out of 3 unlabeled candidates ≥60% of the time). Pass → Stage 1 Build the thin slice with calibration, three chapters, the early Mirror reveal, the Coach agent, and Mahabharata theme toggle. 8–10 weeks. Fail → Stop or Pivot The core premise of the seven-mode franchise does not work. Two options: pivot the Mirror to a post-game analysis tool ("how an AI of you would have played this"), or end the project. Do not build Stage 1 hoping the Mirror improves with story. Decision · After Stage 1 · 10–13 weeks in Do beta players engage with the hook and return? Measured by: ≥30% D1 retention among players who completed the Mirror reveal, ≥10% finish all three chapters, ≥5 unprompted shares from chess accounts at 1500+ Elo, and ≥1 chess content creator engages with the build without paid sponsorship. Pass → Stage 2 Build the complete Mode 1: 19 chapters, 15 characters, multiplayer, all analysis tools, polish. 10–14 weeks. Pricing decision (free vs paid) informed by Stage 1 data. Fail → Iterate or Ship Lean Either iterate on the Mirror, the chapter pacing, or the coach until metrics pass; or ship Stage 1 as the final product with a "future modes" roadmap on the page. Do not build Stage 2 against unfavorable evidence. What this strategy explicitly cuts Cut from the v1 bible — not in Stage 0 or Stage 1 The full nineteen-chapter Mahabharata story arc Twelve of the fifteen named characters (only Vyasa, Yudhishthira, and the Mirror appear in Stage 1) Procedural Blender pipeline for all assets (Stage 1 uses placeholder or AI-generated 2D art) Multiplayer modes — Pilgrim's Path, Sealed Scroll, 2v2 Twin Match The Library of Verses, the Sacred River, the Apprentice's Journal as separate features (only a minimal Vyasa's Review ships in Stage 1) Native iOS, Android, Windows builds (PWA covers all platforms) Audio and music (deferred to Stage 2, AI-generated) The $14.99 purchase model and subscription tier (Stage 0 and Stage 1 are free) The cultural consultant review (Krishna does not appear until Stage 2; the AI agent handles initial scriptwriting then a human reviewer is engaged before Chapter XII script lockdown) Daily Mirror, Echo Tournaments, Style Wars — all deferred to Stage 2 Everything cut from Stage 0 and Stage 1 is restored in Stage 2 if Stage 1 passes. The cuts are not permanent — they are deferrals against unvalidated assumptions. Section Two Stage 0 — The Mirror Prototype. Two weekends. Free. No story, no theme, no characters. A bare web page where a user plays a short calibration, then plays a chess opponent generated from their calibration data. They are asked one question: did that play like you? That is the entire product. It exists to answer the single question that determines whether everything else gets built. Stage 0 — The Mirror Prototype 2 weekends · $0 · Web-only User flow Landing page. One screen. Heading: "Play a chess opponent built from how you play." One button: "Begin." No signup. Anonymous session ID stored in browser. Calibration. Eight tasks. Twelve to fifteen minutes total. Tactical puzzles, opening choices, endgame positions, a short game against Stockfish at depth 5. Progress indicator: "Task 3 of 8." Style vector computed. Browser-side. Eight to ten dimensions, focused on signal-rich features (opening repertoire, time pressure, exchange willingness, blunder phase, tactical motifs missed). Visualized as a simple radar chart with axis labels in plain English: "You play attacking openings. You take time on every move. You miss forks more than pins." Mirror generated. Stockfish at depth 12, perturbation function applied. Displayed to user as: "Your Mirror is ready. It is rated around [Elo]. It plays in your style." First match. Standard chess. Ten minutes per side. The user plays their Mirror. Default piece set: standard Staunton chess pieces. No Mahabharata, no story, no character. Self-recognition check. After the match, present three unlabeled style profiles: the user's Mirror, a generic Maia 1500, and a generic Stockfish at perturbed depth. Ask: "Which one was you?" This is the behavioral test of whether the Mirror is distinguishable. Open feedback. A single textarea: "What did your Mirror get right? What did it get wrong?" Submission optional. Tunable adjustment. Show the player their style vector. Let them reweight any axis up or down ("I am more aggressive than this says"). Regenerate the Mirror. Offer a second match. End screen. Brief: "Thank you. If you would like to play your Mirror again as it learns from more games, leave your email." Optional email capture for the Stage 1 beta cohort. Tech stack — total cost zero Frontend Vite + React + TypeScript. Compiled to a static PWA bundle. Hosting Cloudflare Pages. Free, unlimited bandwidth, global CDN. Chess board react-chessboard + chess.js for move validation. Engine stockfish.wasm client-side. Runs in a Web Worker, depth 12, ~50ms per move on midrange hardware. Storage IndexedDB for calibration data, style vector, session log. No backend in Stage 0. Feedback collection A single Cloudflare Worker (free tier, 100K requests/day) that accepts an anonymous POST and writes to a free Notion database or a Google Sheet via webhook. Analytics Cloudflare Analytics, built-in, free. What ships and what does not In Stage 0 Not in Stage 0 Calibration (8 tasks, ~15 min) Story, narration, Vyasa, Yudhishthira, any character Style vector with 8–10 dimensions Sixteen-dimension full vector One Mirror match Multiple Mirror appearances Self-recognition triangulation Analysis suite, Vyasa's Review Tunable style adjustment Multiplayer, friend invites, matchmaking Standard Staunton pieces Mahabharata-themed pieces, Pandava/Kaurava aesthetic Mobile-friendly responsive web Native iOS/Android builds Anonymous session, optional email Auth, accounts, profiles, subscriptions Pass criteria — behavioral, measured Stage 0 runs as an open beta posted to r/chess , r/IndieDev , Hacker News, and chess Twitter. Target: 200–500 completed sessions in two weeks. Primary: ≥30% of sessions submit unprompted feedback that uses language like "felt like me," "knew my weaknesses," "played my openings," or equivalent. Confirmatory: Self-recognition rate ≥60% on the three-profile triangulation (chance is 33%, so this is a meaningful signal). Indicator of demand: ≥15% of sessions leave an email for the Stage 1 cohort. Quality signal: ≥3 chess players rated 1600+ (verified by Chess.com or Lichess handle voluntarily disclosed) post about the prototype publicly. Kill criterion If feedback consistently uses words like "generic," "Stockfish," "could be anyone," or "boring opponent," the Mirror fails the core test. Do not iterate on the Mirror for another sprint hoping it improves. The pressure tests warned specifically that perturbed Stockfish may produce uncanny-valley chess rather than human-feeling chess. If that prediction holds, the entire seven-mode design depends on a mechanic that does not work. End the project, or pivot to the post-game analysis tool variant ("show me how an AI of myself would have played differently") which is a different and smaller product. Honest probability assessment Based on prior art — Maia's own results, perturbation literature in chess engines, the failure history of personalized chess products — Stage 0 has a meaningful chance of failing. Honest range: Stage 0 passes both primary and confirmatory criteria: ~25–35% That probability is low enough that committing to a twelve-week build before testing is irresponsible. It is high enough that the two-weekend test is worth doing. This is the math of the staged strategy. Section Three Stage 1 — The Hook Test. Eight to ten weeks if Stage 0 passes. The thin slice. A free PWA with calibration, three chapters, the first Mirror reveal at chapter three (not seven), the Coach agent for personalized teaching, and the Mahabharata theme as an optional toggle. Tested with a beta cohort of 200–500 invited users. Built almost entirely with AI coding agents on free-tier infrastructure. Stage 1 — The Hook Test 8–10 weeks · &lt;$200 · Free PWA The thin-slice content Vyasa's Test (calibration). Eight tasks, fifteen minutes. Sets the player's initial style vector and difficulty band. Chapter I — Hastinapura. Vyasa accepts the player. A gentle tutorial match against Vidura (Maia 1300 baseline). Twenty minutes of guided play. Chapter II — Indraprastha. The player meets Yudhishthira and plays him. Maia 1700 with the no-swindle constraint. Twenty-five minutes. Chapter III — The First Mirror. Vyasa reveals the academy's project. The Mirror appears. Match plays. Self-recognition prompt. Post-match Coach intervention to discuss the result. Forty-five minutes total. Coach loop unlocked. After Chapter III, the player can return any time to play their Mirror again, or to run a Coach session (personalized puzzles based on weaknesses identified in matches played). End screen. "More chapters coming. Leave your email." Drives a waitlist for Stage 2. Audience-aware difficulty bands The pressure tests pointed at the split between casual and skilled players. Stage 1 resolves this by detecting the player's level during calibration and offering different content paths. Band Detected Elo (from calibration) Content path Apprentice &lt; 1200 Drona's Gurukul opens first. Pieces taught one at a time through Mahabharata associations. Story chapters use simpler positions. Mirror plays softer. Initiate 1200–1500 Standard path. Calibration → Chapter I → III. Coach intervenes on common motifs. Adept 1500–1800 Skip the basic tutorials. Faster pacing. Section Five The Mirror, Honestly Engineered — addressing the low-Elo signal problem and the perturbed-Stockfish uncanny valley. The pressure tests were specific about two failure modes: style vectors converge at low Elo, and perturbed Stockfish does not feel human. The Mirror's design responds to both with concrete countermeasures. The signal problem at low Elo, and how the Mirror compensates Players rated 1000–1500 blunder enough that their move distributions look statistically similar. A naive sixteen-dimension style vector cannot meaningfully separate them. The countermeasure is not "more dimensions" — it is fewer, higher-signal dimensions, plus a hybrid model. Stage 0 and Stage 1 Mirror — eight high-signal dimensions Opening repertoire (top 3 White, top 3 Black). Most distinctive at any Elo. A 1100 who plays the King's Gambit is not the same as a 1100 who plays the Caro-Kann. Average move time. Some players blitz, some think. Strong signal, easy to capture. Time-pressure blunder rate. Moves played with under five seconds on the clock that lose ≥150 cp. Distinctive by player. Exchange willingness. Proportion of equal trades accepted versus declined. Preferred minor piece. Knight-keeper vs bishop-keeper, derived from trade history. Tactical motif blindness. From calibration: the player's miss rate on forks, pins, skewers, removing-the-defender. The Mirror exploits the weakest two motifs. Endgame technique. Win rate from K+R, K+Q, K+P advantage positions in calibration endgames. Pawn-structure preference. Open positions vs closed, derived from opening choice. The hybrid Mirror — Maia plus perturbed Stockfish, not perturbed Stockfish alone This addresses the uncanny-valley concern directly. A pure perturbed Stockfish oscillates between brilliant and weak in alien ways. The hybrid: Base layer: The Maia model closest to the player's calibration Elo (Maia 1100, 1300, 1500, 1700, or 1900). This provides naturally human move selection. Personalization layer: The Maia move policy is re-ranked by the player's style vector. The vector boosts moves that match the player's opening repertoire and tactical preferences, and demotes moves that the player would not consider. Exploitation layer: In ~20% of positions where the Mirror has an opportunity to test the player's known weakness (a fork the player misses, a pin the player falls for), it switches to a perturbed Stockfish line that creates that position. This three-layer architecture means the Mirror plays like a human at the player's Elo most of the time, plays like the player specifically in opening and middlegame style choices, and occasionally creates positions designed to expose the player's blind spots — which is what makes it feel like a coach as much as an opponent. The tunable Mirror — closing the gap with player feedback After the first Mirror match, the player sees their style vector visualized as a radar. Each axis has a single-line plain-English description: "You play attacking openings." The player can drag any axis up or down to disagree with the model. "I am more careful than this says." The Mirror regenerates immediately. The next match plays with the adjusted vector. This is the answer to the low-Elo signal problem. If the data alone cannot distinguish two 1200-rated players, the player's own self-knowledge can. Co-creating the Mirror with explicit feedback turns a unilateral inference problem into a collaborative model. It also makes the Mirror feel like a conversation rather than a verdict. Self-recognition test — the only behavioral check that matters After a Mirror match, present three unlabeled opponents in card form, each described by their style vector: the player's own Mirror, a generic Maia at the player's Elo, and a different real player's Mirror selected from the cohort. The player picks "which one was you." Chance baseline is 33%. The acceptance criterion is ≥60% correct identification. This is the cleanest measurement of whether the Mirror is meaningfully personalized. Section Six The Coach — a teaching agent that engages every player after every game. Separate from the Mirror. The Mirror is the player; the Coach is a teacher. The Coach serves the requirement that the game help beginners learn and skilled players improve — a requirement Chess.com, Chessable, and existing chess products meet only partially because their teaching is generic. The Coach is personalized to the player's specific games. The Coach's responsibilities Post-game intervention. After every game (story, Mirror, free play, or future multiplayer), the Coach appears in character voice and selects one specific moment from the game to discuss. The moment is chosen by Stockfish's analysis — the highest centipawn-loss blunder or the largest missed brilliancy. Personalized puzzle generation. The Coach identifies the player's two weakest tactical motifs (from the running tally across all games) and generates a five-puzzle drill set targeting those specific motifs. Puzzles are drawn from a curated puzzle database keyed by motif tag. Lesson delivery. When a player has lost the same way three times — the same opening trap, the same endgame failure, the same time-pressure blunder pattern — the Coach surfaces a focused lesson with three example positions and a one-paragraph explanation. Adaptive difficulty. The Coach tracks the player's progress on weak motifs and gradually increases puzzle difficulty as accuracy improves. Mastered motifs are deprioritized; new weaknesses are picked up. Encouragement and pacing. Sessions never exceed twenty minutes by default. The Coach prompts breaks. This is a deliberate anti-pattern against chess platforms that optimize for time-on-task. Technical implementation Move analysis Stockfish at depth 14 over every move in every game. Classification by standard centipawn-loss thresholds. Motif tagging via a lightweight tactical classifier (open source). Coach voice generation Google Gemini 1.5 Flash via the free API (1,500 requests/day at Stage 1 scale). One call per Coach intervention. Prompts engineered against a character profile (default: Drona, the teacher from the Mahabharata; alternate voice: a neutral "Coach" persona for players using the Standard theme). Puzzle bank Lichess open puzzle database (4M+ puzzles, public domain). Filtered by motif tag, rating, and themes. No proprietary content needed. Personal weakness model Local SQLite (synced to Supabase) tracking the player's history of motif failures with rolling counts and decay. Daily limit One Coach intervention per game per player, capped at five Coach sessions per day in free tier. Hard limit enforced client-side; soft prompt: "Drona suggests rest. Come back tomorrow." Sample Coach interaction (Apprentice band, after a fork blunder) After the Yudhishthira match · Coach: Drona "Move sixteen. You moved the knight from f3 to d4. The knight was attacked by Yudhishthira's bishop, but the bishop was also defending his queen. You did not see that your own bishop on g5 could have captured the queen instead. This is a fork pattern. It is the third time in your last ten games. We will work on this now." [ Five fork puzzles offered. Each ~30 seconds. Drona returns at the end with feedback. ] Why the Coach is not optional The Mirror tests whether the game's central artistic vision works. The Coach tests whether the game has a reason to exist for the chess audience that is not story-driven. Chess.com keeps players because it helps them improve. Without a serious teaching system, this product is a curiosity. With one, it has a chance to compete on its own terms — not as a Chess.com replacement, but as a complement that knows the player personally and teaches accordingly. The pressure tests said: this product sits between markets and satisfies neither. The Coach is how the chess-improvement market is served without abandoning the artistic vision. What it feels like to be inside this game. A great game is a place. Before we describe characters or chapters, we describe the air the player breathes when they open the app — the temperature, the colors, the sound of the wood when a piece is set down, the slant of light at four in the afternoon. The age The Mahabharata as a setting is not historical India and not mythic India — it is the India of an undated age before the iron of the Kuru war, where the Pandavas have just built their capital at Indraprastha and the wheel of dharma is turning toward Kurukshetra. The player arrives in this world as Vatsala, a young chaturanga apprentice from a village too small to have a name on any map. They carry nothing but a wooden board and a cloth roll of pieces. The light is always one of three: warm oil-lamp gold for indoor scenes, the cool blue of dawn for matches with Arjuna, the orange dust of dusk for the battlefield chapters. There is no harsh midday sun in this game. We never see noon. The world feels perpetually on the edge of something — either becoming or ending. The sound (deferred to Stage 2; described here so the future build understands the brief) When music is added, it will be sparse. Indian classical raga, but only single instruments — sitar alone, sarangi alone, bansuri alone. No orchestral swells. The sound of a chess piece set on wood is the loudest sound in this game. Footsteps on stone. A door closing. A river. The wind in the forest of exile. The space around the board This is the design decision that separates MIRROR from every other chess product: the chess board is never the whole screen. The board occupies roughly sixty percent of the playable area. The remaining space shows where you are. When you play Yudhishthira, you see the pavilion at Indraprastha behind the board — open sides, white marble floor, attendants in the background. When you play Bhima, you see the practice yard, dust, weights, wrestling pits. When you play the Mirror, you see a riverbank at dusk and a figure that mirrors your every gesture. This is not decoration. The setting tells the player who they are playing in a way that piece tunings cannot. A player who has fought Bhima will remember the smell of the wrestling yard. A player who has lost to Karna will remember the bowstring across his chest. The world is the storytelling. The seven worlds Mode 1 is one of seven planned story modes. Act I — The Apprentice's Awakening Six chapters. The player arrives in the world, learns its rules, meets the three eldest Pandavas, and witnesses the dice game that breaks everything. The act ends with the Pandavas exiled and the player at a crossroads. Chapter I · Calibration · 15–18 min · Vyasa The Test by the Banyan Tree In which an old sage finds an apprentice he was not looking for. The game opens on Vatsala, the player's character, in a small village at the edge of the Vatsala forest. They are alone with a wooden chessboard on a low stone, working through a position by themselves. A traveler stops to watch. The traveler is old, dressed in saffron, carrying nothing but a walking stick and a satchel of palm-leaf manuscripts. He sits down across from the player without asking. "Show me how you play," he says. The calibration begins. Eight tasks, framed as Vyasa's examination of the apprentice — a tactical puzzle in which the player chooses among three candidate moves, an opening choice with six possibilities, an endgame to solve, a tactical race, a moral chess position, a repertoire prompt, and one short game against Vyasa himself. Each task is preceded by one or two lines of his dialogue establishing what the task reveals. At the end he closes his manuscript and stands. "You are not what I expected," he says. "I expected nothing in particular. So that is consistent. Come with me. There is something I want you to see before the war begins." Mechanic Calibration logged. Style vector computed and persisted. The Mirror begins training the moment the player makes their first move. Chapter II · Story · 25 min · Vidura Hastinapura In which the apprentice sees the great city for the first time and learns what is at stake. Vyasa brings the player into Hastinapura through the eastern gate at dawn. The city is rich and tense. Banners of the Kuru house hang from every parapet. Vyasa walks the player through the palace, past the throne where Bhishma sits silently, past the courtyard where Drona is teaching a group of younger boys, past a high balcony where Duryodhana is watching them without expression. They arrive at Vidura's library. Vidura is the wise minister of the Kuru court — a brother of Bhishma in spirit if not in blood. He has been told, somehow, that the player is coming. He sets up a board and begins to speak. The match is the tutorial match. Vidura plays gentle chess on purpose and talks the player through it. He explains the political situation as the pieces move. Two cousins. Two claims to the throne. One inheritance that was promised and then taken back. A king who is blind in both eyes (Dhritarashtra) and a queen who chose to blindfold herself in solidarity (Gandhari). The Pandavas have a new capital at Indraprastha because the elders gave them half a kingdom to keep the peace. The Kauravas hate this arrangement. The dice game is coming. "You will play all of them before you leave," Vidura says, taking a piece carelessly that the player could have defended. "I hope you remember me as the kind one. I am the only kind one in the family." Mechanic Standard match, Maia 1500. Most players win. The first match a player likely wins becomes a confidence anchor. Chapter III · Story · 30 min · Yudhishthira Indraprastha In which the apprentice meets a king who refuses to trick him, and learns what that costs. Vyasa and the player travel south. The streets are full of artisans putting the finishing touches on a city that has not yet decided what it wants to be. Yudhishthira receives them in a pavilion of white marble. He is younger than the player expected — only mid-thirties — and quieter. He pours water for the player himself. Then he gestures at the board. "My uncle Vidura said you would come. He said you played well. I would like to see for myself." The match begins. Yudhishthira plays a calm, principled chess. The player tries the kinds of tactical tricks that worked against Vidura. None of them work. Yudhishthira sees every threat and refuses to fall for any of them. When the player offers a swindle — a piece that looks like a free capture but loses in three moves — Yudhishthira simply declines it. He does not take pieces he cannot keep. Chapter IV · Story · 25 min · Bhima The Wrestler's Welcome In which the apprentice loses one piece of dignity and gains a friend. Engineered honestly, including its known limits and the countermeasures designed to address them. A perturbed Stockfish weighted by sixteen dimensions creates uncanny chess. A pure Maia plays human but does not feel like any specific human. The Mirror is neither — it is a three-layer hybrid designed against the two known failure modes pressure tests have flagged. The three-layer architecture Layer 1 — Maia base The foundation. The Mirror starts from Maia tuned to the player's calibration Elo (Maia 1100, 1300, 1500, 1700, or 1900 depending on detected band). Maia is published by CSSLab and trained to predict human moves. This guarantees that, at baseline, the Mirror plays moves a human at the player's level would actually consider. No uncanny valley at this layer. Layer 2 — Style-vector reranking Maia returns a probability distribution over candidate moves. The personalization layer reranks that distribution using the player's eight-dimension style vector — boosting moves that match the player's opening repertoire and tactical preferences, demoting moves the player would not consider. This is the layer that makes the Mirror feel personal. Layer 3 — Opportunistic Stockfish exploitation In roughly 20% of positions — specifically, positions where the player has a known weak tactical motif (fork, pin, skewer, removing-the-defender) — the Mirror switches to a perturbed Stockfish line that creates exactly the position the player tends to misjudge. This is the layer that makes the Mirror feel like it is trying to test the player. The eight style-vector dimensions (Stage 1 — Stage 2 expands to sixteen) Opening repertoire (top 3 White, top 3 Black) Average move time Time-pressure blunder rate (moves with under 5s clock that lose ≥150 cp) Exchange willingness (proportion of equal trades accepted) Preferred minor piece (knight-keeper vs bishop-keeper) Tactical motif blindness (miss rate on forks, pins, skewers from calibration) Endgame technique (win rate from K+R, K+Q advantage in calibration) Pawn-structure preference (open vs closed positions) The tunable Mirror After the first Mirror match, the player sees their style vector visualized as a radar with plain-English axis labels. "You play attacking openings. You take time on every move. You miss forks more than pins." The player can drag any axis up or down to disagree. The Mirror regenerates immediately and offers a second match. This is the answer to the low-Elo signal problem — the data alone cannot always distinguish two 1200-rated players, but the player's own self-knowledge can. Self-recognition test — the only behavioral check that matters After the first Mirror match, the player sees three unlabeled style profiles: their own Mirror, a generic Maia at their Elo, and a different player's Mirror from the cohort. The player is asked "which one was you." Chance baseline is 33%. Acceptance threshold is 60%. This is the cleanest measurement of whether the Mirror is meaningfully personalized. Section Five The Coach Why players will return tomorrow. The retention mechanic that pressure tests correctly identified as the missing piece in the previous documents. If the Mirror is the hook — the reason the game gets shared — the Coach is the reason the game gets opened again. Mirror is novelty without Coach. Mirror plus Coach is a daily product. What the Coach does After every game — story, Mirror, multiplayer, free practice — Drona appears (in Mahabharata theme; a neutral "Coach" character in Standard theme) and selects one specific moment from the game to discuss. The moment is chosen by Stockfish's analysis: the highest centipawn-loss blunder or the largest missed brilliancy. The Coach does five things: Calls out one specific moment. Not the whole game. Not a summary. One move, named, in character voice. Tags the tactical motif. Fork, pin, skewer, hanging piece, missed defensive resource. Tagged via a small tactical classifier running over the game's Stockfish analysis. Tracks the pattern over time. If the player has missed forks five times in their last ten games, the Coach says so. "This is the fifth time. Sit. We will fix this now." Serves personalized puzzles. A drill of 3-5 puzzles targeting that specific motif, drawn from the Lichess open puzzle database (CC0, 4 million+ puzzles). Adapts difficulty. The player's accuracy on each motif is tracked. Mastered motifs are deprioritized. New weaknesses are picked up. A sample Coach interaction After Chapter III · Loss to Yudhishthira · Coach: Drona "Move sixteen. You moved the knight from f3 to d4. The knight was attacked by Yudhishthira's bishop, but the bishop was also defending his queen. You did not see that your own bishop on g5 could have captured the queen instead. This is a fork pattern. It is the third time in your last ten games. We will work on this now." [Five fork puzzles offered. Each ~30 seconds. Drona returns at the end with feedback. The whole intervention is six to eight minutes.] Why the Coach is the answer to the pressure tests Three pressure tests said: the Mirror is novelty, not habit. They were right. A player who plays the Mirror once and never returns has not learned anything; they have seen a parlor trick. The Coach turns this into a real loop: The Mirror makes the player lose. The Coach explains exactly why and offers to help. The player improves on a specific motif. The player plays the Mirror again, sharper. The Mirror has also improved (more data). The cycle continues. This is the same loop that powers Chess.com's lessons feature and Duolingo's spaced-repetition language model. We are not inventing a category. We are bringing the loop to a product that also has a story. Implementation Analysis engine Stockfish at depth 14 over every move in every game. Classification by standard centipawn-loss thresholds (>50 cp inaccuracy, >150 cp mistake, >300 cp blunder). Motif classifier Lightweight pattern detector. For each blunder, tag the motif. Open-source detectors available; we extend if needed. Coach voice generation Google Gemini 1.5 Flash via free API (1,500 requests/day, 15 RPM at Stage 1 scale this is comfortably under limit). Prompt engineered against Drona character profile. One call per Coach intervention. Puzzle bank Lichess open puzzle database (CC0). 4M+ puzzles filtered by motif tag, rating, theme. No proprietary content needed. Personal weakness model Local SQLite synced to Supabase. Tracks the player's history of motif failures with rolling counts and decay. Daily limit One Coach intervention per game. Capped at 5 Coach sessions per day on free tier. Soft prompt: "Drona suggests rest. Come back tomorrow." The Daily Mirror — the habit loop After the player completes Chapter VII (the first Mirror reveal), a new feature unlocks: the Daily Mirror. Once every 24 hours, the player can play one match against their current Mirror. The Mirror updates daily based on the previous day's games. Win streak tracked. Skipping a day does not break the streak; losing does. This is the Wordle pattern applied to chess. One match per day. Ten to fifteen minutes. Free, no friction, no signup beyond the original session. The Daily Mirror is the answer to "would they return within 24 hours" — the pressure test's actual ask. If the Daily Mirror works, MIRROR is a product. If it does not, MIRROR is an art piece. Either is acceptable. Only one is a product. Section Six For Beginners and Masters Alike One game, two doors.
